## Determining Whether Copyright Was Renewed

For a work first published in the United States before 1964, copyright generally had an initial term of 28 years. To continue protection, the copyright had to be renewed during the 28th year.

If copyright was not properly renewed, the work entered the public domain when its initial term expired. Works published from 1964 onward received automatic renewal, so this investigation applies principally to works published from 1931 through 1963.

[Copyright Office explanation of renewal](https://www.copyright.gov/vcc/card-guide.html)

### First Steps

1. **Examine the work itself.**

   Record as much identifying information as possible:

   - Exact title
   - Author or creator
   - Publisher
   - Publication date
   - Copyright-notice date
   - Copyright claimant named in the notice
   - Original copyright registration number, if present

   Be sure that you are investigating the particular edition or version you intend to use.

2. **For a book, search the Stanford Copyright Renewal Database.**

   [Stanford Copyright Renewal Database](https://exhibits.stanford.edu/copyrightrenewals)

   This database indexes U.S. Class A renewal registrations for books published in the United States from 1923 through 1963.

   Search using several possible access points:

   - Title
   - Author
   - Publisher
   - Copyright claimant
   - Original registration number

   Allow for spelling variations, title changes, subtitles, and corporate-name changes.

3. **Search the U.S. Copyright Office records.**

   [Copyright Public Records Portal](https://www.copyright.gov/public-records/)

   The portal provides access to several collections of records:

   - **Catalog of Copyright Entries:** registrations and renewals from 1891–1978
   - **Virtual Card Catalog:** Copyright Office records from 1870–1977
   - **Copyright Public Records System:** electronic records covering 1978 onward

   Stanford is especially convenient for books. For photographs, music, films, periodicals, artwork, and other classes of works, consult the relevant Copyright Office records.

4. **Search more than one way.**

   Failure to find the exact title is not sufficient evidence that copyright was not renewed.

   Search using:

   - Author or creator
   - Copyright claimant
   - Publisher
   - Alternate or shortened titles
   - Original registration number
   - Likely renewal year and adjacent years

   Renewal registration numbers generally begin with **R** or **RE**.

5. **Document a negative search.**

   If no renewal is found, record:

   - Databases and catalogs searched
   - Years searched
   - Names and title variants searched
   - Registration numbers searched
   - Results obtained

   Finding a renewal establishes that copyright continued. Failing to find one does not immediately establish that it did not. A responsible researcher should document a reasonably exhaustive search before concluding that a work entered the public domain.

### When the Result Remains Uncertain

The Copyright Office cautions that even a careful search may not produce a conclusive answer. It can conduct a paid search and provide a written report.

See:

[How to Investigate the Copyright Status of a Work — Copyright Office Circular 22](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ22.pdf)

### Important Qualifications

This simplified renewal test works best for works first published in the United States.

Foreign works may present additional complications, including copyright restored under the Uruguay Round Agreements Act.

A researcher should also distinguish the original work from separately protected material added later, such as:

- Revised text
- Introductions
- Annotations
- Translations
- Illustrations
- Photographs
- New arrangements
- Later editions

The fact that the original work is in the public domain does not necessarily mean that every element of a later edition is also in the public domain.
